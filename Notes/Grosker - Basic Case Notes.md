  ### Brief Overview of the case   

  case was decided by the U.S. Supreme Court on June 27, 2005, and the Court ruled unanimously against Grokster and StreamCast    

  Streamcast and Grokster were peer to peer software companies that    
  allowed users' computers to communicate directly with other users' computers and exchange files.   

  MGM Studios among other artists, companies, and producers sued streamcast, grokster, and other p2p software companies for copyright infringement   

  Most shared files by users were movies and music   

  MGM studios et al. argued that p2p software companies intentionally built a product that would allow users to copyright.   
    by choosing to sue the company and not the users (which were far too many), mgm engaged in secondary copyright liability   

  Grokster based its argument on the Sony Corp of America v Universal studios court decision (1984) that a company    
  isn't automatically responsible merely because its technology can be used for copyright infringement    
  if the technology also has substantial legitimate uses.   
  
   the lower courts initially agreed with grokster.   
   The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals had ruled in Grokster's favor based largely on the Sony principle.   

   Once the case went to the supreme court, evidence of Grokster's copyright intentions and advertisement as a napster alternative, were exposed, changing the   
   outcome for the case.   

   The inducement theory (a critical outcome for the case and for all future cases):    
   - A company can be liable when it distributes technology with the purpose of   
     encouraging people to infringe copyrights, demonstrated through its statements or actions   

  The Court explained that merely knowing your technology can be used illegally isn't enough.   
  Instead, there needs to be evidence of purposeful conduct aimed at encouraging infringement.
