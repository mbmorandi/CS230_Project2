We think Grokster’s overall technological setup isn't automatically bad, but the business definitely had a duty to look into how people were using their program and whether it was causing real issues. P2P file sharing is really a dual-use tech since the exact same framework can transfer legal files or spread damaging stuff. Having zero restrictions gives users quick access, ease of use, and total freedom, but it also opens the door to copyright issues, computer viruses, and illegal documents. Thinking back to whether complete liberty should be favored, unlimited freedom shouldn’t be prioritized over the possibility of putting others in harm. Copyright is meant to shield original works, and letting everyone copy media without paying means producers miss out on revenue after spending so much time and money creating it. When looking at the legal side, the Supreme Court decision in MGM Studios, Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd. pointed out that Grokster and StreamCast actively encouraged copyright infringement and didn't bother putting any safeguards in place (MGM Studios, Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd., 2005). Looking at our group discussions, Mursal, Misha, and Azhan had mostly similar viewpoints on business responsibility with slight differences. Mursal felt businesses need to be held accountable if the damage is predictable, whereas Misha argued they must adapt when data proves their innovation is causing harm. Both ideas make sense, but we also have to ask: how big is the issue? From a utilitarian view, you have to weigh the bad against the good that comes out of the technology. While unrestricted downloads give users quick benefits, massive piracy harms creators and forces legitimate consumers to deal with the fallout. Under contract theory, a business needs to evaluate if the guidelines behind their product are fair to everyone involved. This means adding a few boundaries is ethical if it shields creator rights without destroying the core value of p2p tech. Honestly, Grokster could have handled things much better by updating their terms of service, warning and educating about copyright, working alongside copyright holders, or converting into a legal streaming subscription. Taking these steps would cut down on malicious activity while maintaining the easy access that made p2p so popular in the first place.
