
We think Grokster’s overall technological setup isn't automatically bad, but the business definitely had a duty to look into how people were using their program and whether it was causing real issues. P2P file sharing is really a dual-use tech since the exact same framework can transfer legal files or spread damaging stuff. Having zero restrictions gives users quick access, ease of use, and total freedom, but it also opens the door to copyright issues, computer viruses, and illegal documents. Thinking back to whether complete liberty should be favored, unlimited freedom shouldn’t be prioritized over the possibility of putting others in harm. Copyright is meant to shield original works, and letting everyone copy media without paying means producers miss out on revenue after spending so much time and money creating it. When looking at the legal side, the Supreme Court decision in MGM Studios, Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd. pointed out that Grokster and StreamCast actively encouraged copyright infringement and didn't bother putting any safeguards in place (MGM Studios, Inc. v. Grokster, Ltd., 2005). Looking at our group discussions, Mursal, Misha, and Azhan had mostly similar viewpoints on business responsibility with slight differences. Mursal felt businesses need to be held accountable if the damage is predictable, whereas Misha argued they must adapt when data proves their innovation is causing harm. Both ideas make sense, but we also have to ask: how big is the issue? From a utilitarian view, you have to weigh the bad against the good that comes out of the technology. While unrestricted downloads give users quick benefits, massive piracy harms creators and forces legitimate consumers to deal with the fallout. Under contract theory, a business needs to evaluate if the guidelines behind their product are fair to everyone involved. This means adding a few boundaries is ethical if it shields creator rights without destroying the core value of p2p tech. Honestly, Grokster could have handled things much better by updating their terms of service, warning and educating about copyright, working alongside copyright holders, or converting into a legal streaming subscription. Taking these steps would cut down on malicious activity while maintaining the easy access that made p2p so popular in the first place.

The MGM v Grokster case touches directly on a number of articles in the Association for Computing Machinery’s Code of Ethics. Both article 1.5 [1] “Respect the work required to produce new ideas, inventions, creative works, and computing artifacts.” and 2.3 [1] “Know and respect existing rules pertaining to professional work.” pertain to the expected respect of copyright law and intellectual property. These articles are often applied to issues involving unauthorized use of codebases or intellectual property by companies or individuals but their broader concerns reflect the ethical importance placed on the respect of intellectual property and the laws which govern it.

While these articles clearly establish the ACM’s support of intellectual property [1], the most damning evidence in this case which eventually led to the court’s ruling, pertained not merely to copyright infringement but also to the the motives, actions, and communications of the companies and their leadership. The evidence presented to the court detailing the companies’ internal communications and marketing strategies are in direct opposition to Article 1.3 [1] “Be honest and trustworthy”. The mismatch between the ways Grokster and Streamcast attempted to defend themselves and their internal communications and actions was in direct conflict with this article.

Article 3.4 [1] makes these ethical concerns even clearer when it calls for leadership to “Articulate, apply, and support policies and processes that reflect the principles of the Code.” It was this lack of ethical direction by leadership and in fact their internally flouted opposition to these principles that led the court to rule against them.

The ACM was particularly concerned with this high profile case and the ways in which it could shape technological innovations and freedoms. The United States Public Policy Committee of the ACM in conjunction with sixty law professors submitted an amici curiae brief to the Supreme Court elucidating their concerns. The brief very clearly did not support the actions of the defendants but instead focused on the importance of maintaining technological freedoms [2]. The authors were afraid that an overly broad standard of secondary liability would deter growth and innovation [2]. These concerns seem to have been held by the court as well since the opinion by Justice Souter makes it clear that the defendants were not ruled against because of the underlying technologies they had created, but instead because of their clear encouragement of their technology to be used illegally [3]. And it is these actions that exist in greatest opposition to the ACM’s Code of Ethics.
