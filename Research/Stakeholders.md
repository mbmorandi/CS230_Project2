# Mursal Hamidy's Research 
## Major Stakeholders
### MGM and the other copyright owners: 
MGM represented a large group of motion-picture studios, recording companies, songwriters, and music publishers. These parties owned copyrights in movies and music that users shared without authorization. They wanted to protect their intellectual property, maintain control over the distribution of their work, and prevent financial harm. They sued Grokster and StreamCast, requested damages and an injunction, and appealed after the lower courts ruled for the software companies [1].

### Grokster:
Grokster distributed free peer to peer file sharing software that used the FastTrack network. The company earned money by displaying advertisements to users. More users created more advertising opportunities. Grokster argued that its technology had legal uses and that it did not control the files exchanged by individual users. However, MGM presented evidence that Grokster tried to attract former Napster users and promoted access to popular copyrighted material [1].

### StreamCast Networks:
StreamCast distributed Morpheus, which used Gnutella peer to peer technology. Like Grokster, StreamCast earned advertising revenue from a large user base. Evidence showed that StreamCast wanted to become an alternative to Napster and actively marketed its software to former Napster users. StreamCast defended itself by arguing that its decentralized network did not give it control over individual file transfers [1].

### Users of Grokster and Morpheus:
Users downloaded the software for free and exchanged files directly with other users. Many used it to share copyrighted music and movies without authorization. However, the technology could also be used legally to distribute authorized works, public-domain materials, government documents, and other noninfringing content [1].

### Musicians, filmmakers, and other creators:
Creators relied on copyright protections to control and receive compensation for their work. Unauthorized sharing could reduce their control over distribution and potentially affect their income. Artists and entertainment-industry organizations therefore had an interest in making the software companies responsible for encouraging infringement.

### Technology developers and companies:
The decision affected developers whose products can be used for both legal and illegal purposes. Technology organizations were concerned that making developers automatically responsible for every misuse could discourage innovation. However, allowing companies to intentionally encourage illegal activity without consequences could harm copyright owners. The case attempted to distinguish neutral technology development from purposeful encouragement of infringement.

### Electronic Frontier Foundation:
The Electronic Frontier Foundation represented StreamCast and supported protecting technological innovation. Because EFF represented one of the defendants, its commentary may reflect that position.
