# Mursal Hamidy’s Research
## Technology
Grokster and StreamCast distributed decentralized peer-to-peer, or P2P, file-sharing software. Peer-to-peer means that users’ computers communicate and exchange files directly instead of sending every file through a central server operated by the software company.
Grokster used FastTrack technology. A search request could go to a powerful user computer called a “supernode,” which maintained a temporary index of available files. After locating a file, the requesting user downloaded it directly from another user’s computer.
StreamCast’s Morpheus program used Gnutella technology. A user’s search request traveled between connected computers until matching files were located. The requested file was then downloaded directly from another user.
This decentralized design was important because Grokster and StreamCast did not control a central list of files or participate directly in each transfer. This made the services different from Napster, which had used a centralized index. The lower courts believed the decentralized design limited the companies’ knowledge of and control over specific acts of infringement [2][3].
The Supreme Court did not declare P2P technology illegal. It recognized that it had legitimate uses. The Court instead focused on whether Grokster and StreamCast distributed and promoted their products with the intention of encouraging copyright infringement [1].

## Case Background
MGM and other copyright owners filed suit in federal court in 2001. They claimed that Grokster and StreamCast should be indirectly responsible because users employed their software to copy and distribute copyrighted music and movies without authorization.
In 2003, the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California granted partial summary judgment to Grokster and StreamCast. The court concluded that the current versions of their software were capable of substantial legal uses. It also found that the companies did not have enough control over their decentralized networks to be liable under the contributory- and vicarious-infringement theories presented at that stage [3].
In 2004, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed the District Court’s ruling. It relied heavily on Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios, the Betamax case. Under Sony, distributing a product capable of substantial legal uses does not automatically make its distributor responsible when customers misuse it [2].
The Supreme Court considered the case in 2005. It agreed that a product’s lawful uses remain important, but decided that the lower courts had not properly considered evidence of intentional encouragement. The Court established the inducement rule: a company may be liable when it distributes a product with the purpose of encouraging copyright infringement and takes affirmative steps to promote that infringement [1].
The Court identified evidence that Grokster and StreamCast targeted former Napster users, earned more advertising revenue from increased use, and did not develop meaningful measures to reduce infringement. The Supreme Court unanimously vacated the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and sent the case back for further proceedings. Therefore, it is more accurate to say that MGM prevailed at the Supreme Court than to say the Supreme Court awarded MGM damages [1].

## Questions and Conflicting Information
1. Should the case be described as a complete MGM victory? 
The Supreme Court ruled in MGM’s favor on the inducement issue, vacated the Ninth Circuit decision, and remanded the case. It did not calculate damages during this decision.

2. How much illegal activity occurred? 
MGM’s study suggested that nearly 90 percent of files available through FastTrack were copyrighted, but Grokster and StreamCast disputed the methodology and argued that some copying was authorized [1].

3. Does failing to build a filter prove illegal intent? 
The Supreme Court said that failure to filter would not establish liability by itself. It became important when considered with other evidence of intentional encouragement [1].
