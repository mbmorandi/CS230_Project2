## District Court

Court: United States District Court for the Central District of California.  
Judge: Stephen V. Wilson.  

Plantiffs: MGM Studios, Paramount, Sony, Disney, Universal Studios, and other artists/producers 
Defendants: Grokster and Streamcast networks (Morpheus) 
Reason: users were sharing copyrighted material using p2p software 

The big question: Should Grokster and StreamCast themselves be legally responsible for copyright infringement committed by people using their software? 
Grokster and Streamcast argument: “We provide the software. We don't control what individual users decide to do with it.” 

Understanding the technology was important to the judge's decision 

**p2p software worked differently than Napster** 
p2p software involved people searching for nearby computers that had the file they wanted. They could then go to this computer user and request a copy. The file did not travel 
through a computer that grokster owned. 

**Some background information about napster** 
- pioneered p2p 
- established 1999 
-  had a centralized Index: Unlike later decentralized networks, Napster used a central directory server to keep track of the files currently online and available from  connected users. 
-  Napster shutdown in 2001 and went bankrupt in 2002 
-  Napster helped operate the place where the infringement occurred unlike Grokster. 

The key difference between Napster and Grokster played a massive role in the judges decision 

Without a doubt, the entertainment companies were able to establish that users were violating copyright laws. 
However, that did not automatically mean Grokster and StreamCast were responsible for what those users did. 

The court next had to determine whether the companies could be held indirectly responsible. 

The first major theory was called contributory infringement (helping someone else infringe on copyright laws) 

Two major things generally needed to be shown: 
1. The defendant knew about the infringement. 
2. The defendant materially contributed to the infringement. 

The court aknowledged that grokter and streamcast were aware that some of its users were copyrighting. but this was not enough 

Sony's Betamax VCR. 
- VCR's could be used to copyright films 
- some people used them to do so, but there were still other valid legal applicaitons for a blank tape. 
- A product that is capable of substantial legal uses cannot automatically make its distributor responsible simply because some customers use it illegally. 

The court believed this ruling also applied to grokster and streamcast 
the court concluded that Grokster and Morpheus had substantial non-infringing uses. 

Because grokster and streamcast had a decentralized p2p software, they did not receive information related to copyrighted information from its user base. 
 
The entertainment companies also argued that Grokster and StreamCast materially contributed to infringement. 

The judge compared Grokster and StreamCast to companies selling things like: 
VCRs 
photocopiers 

Therefore, the court ruled that Grokster and StreamCast were not liable for contributory infringement under the law being applied at this stage of the case. 

Entertainment industry claimed Grokster and StreamCast were responsible through something called vicarious infringement. 

vicarious infringement can happen when someone: 
1. financially benefits from copyright infringement, and 
2. has the right and ability to control or supervise the infringement. 

To this the court asked two questions: 
1. did grokster financially benefit from copyrighting? 
    - yes, but through the money they made with ads. 
2. could grokster control or supervise the infrindgement 
    - Grokster and StreamCast operated differently than napster. 
    - Their users exchanged files across decentralized networks. 
    - Grokster used the FastTrack network. 
    - Morpheus used the Gnutella network. 
    - Grokster did not control FastTrack, and StreamCast did not control the decentralized Gnutella network. 
    - Once users had the software, the companies could not simply watch every transfer and remove particular users in the same way Napster could. 

The entertainment companies made another interesting argument. 
They said that even if Grokster did not currently control the network, the company could redesign its software to make copyright infringement harder. 
“You designed the software. You could design it differently so people couldn't pirate as easily.” 
The court rejected this as a basis for vicarious liability. 

The fact that a company could theoretically redesign its product to make illegal use more difficult did not mean the company currently controlled its users. 

the court believed that existing copyright law did not go far enough to make them legally responsible under the theories MGM presented. 
The judge essentially said: 
Courts should not expand copyright law just because new technology has created a difficult situation. 
If existing copyright law was inadequate for decentralized peer-to-peer technology, the judge believed Congress might need to change the law. 

The court agreed that users were illegally sharing copyrighted material.
The court also agreed that Grokster and StreamCast financially benefited from having a large user base that was attracted in part by copyrighted material. 
However, under the copyright rules the court applied, those facts were not enough. 
The court concluded that Grokster and StreamCast were not sufficiently involved in or able to control individual acts of infringement to be held liable for contributory or  vicarious copyright infringement.

Summary: MGM studios and other entertainment companies/artists sued Grokster and Streamcast for copyright infringement. p2p software companies argued that they are not responsible for how people use their application. The court examined how the technology worked specifically focusing on the decentralized component that distringuished it from Napster. Napster was a former tech company that also marketed p2p software, but theirs was centralized, meaning napster had access to data regarding p2p software transfers. After discussing the technology, the court shifted the focus to **secondary copyright infringement** the court accepted that users were copyrighting information using Grokster and streamcast. The court then asked if they were aware, which they were aware of, genreally speaking. The court then examined Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios, a case that previously established that a tech company is not responsible for users use of the tech. Grokster and streamcast are generally aware of infringement but not aware of particular instances of it. MGM then argued that the software itself enabled infrindgemnt -> court replied that G + S are different from napster (decentralized). Contributory copyright infrindgemnt is not proven. MGM -> vicarious infringement. court cannot establish vicarious infrigment because while they benefit financially from copyright infringment, they do not supervise these activities. After both of these arguments were presented, mgm argued that G + S should change their software to prevent these issues. Once again, the decentralized aspect of their tech does not support this request. The case concludes once the judge discusses the possibility that grokster and streamcast purposely designed their software to avoid liability.
